The calendar takes a sixth of the portfolio

The withdrawal debate gets argued as a rate: 4 percent or 3.5, guardrails or flat. The rate decides how much leaves the portfolio over a year. The calendar decides what each dollar of it costs while it waits. Run the same 1.2 million, the same 60,000 a year, and the same 5 percent return two ways, one spending it all in January and one in twelve monthly pieces, and thirty years later the balances differ by 199,317. Nothing but the timing moved.

Front-loaded, the year looks like this: 60,000 out on January 2, the remaining 1,140,000 compounds for eleven full months, and the account ends the year at 1,140,000 x 1.05 = 1,197,000. The whole year's draw sat out the year's growth, which prices the convenience at 60,000 x 0.05 = 3,000 in year one alone.

Back-loaded, 5,000 leaves at each month end, after the month's interest posts. Monthly interest on the starting balance is 1,200,000 x 0.05 / 12 = 5,000, which is exactly the draw, so the account ends year one at 1,200,000, the same number it started with, and it ends every year there for thirty years. The account funds each month's spending with the interest it earned that same month, and the principal never has to cover the bill.

Thirty years out, the front-loaded plan holds 1,200,000 - 3,000 x ((1.05^30 - 1) / 0.05) = 1,200,000 - 199,317 = 1,000,683. Every year's January draw costs the 3,000 that money would have earned that year, and each 3,000 keeps compounding to the end: thirty of them, stacked and grown, are the whole gap. The back-loaded plan still holds 1,200,000. The gap is a sixth of the starting balance, 16.6 percent, earned by no fund, no forecast, and no extra risk, just by letting each dollar spend its last month working instead of its first.

The calendar cuts both ways, and a crash year flips it. Take the full 60,000 on January 2 and a 30 percent drop that year never touches the money, 18,000 of draw kept whole, which is why the playbook that preaches spending late preaches spending early the year the market falls. Ordering is one coin. Return sequence is the side everyone argues about; spending sequence is the side you control without a forecast.

Survival checks first, withdrawal math second, and the ordering of the checks themselves third: that stack is the editorial spine of Firenomics, where the guides live at firenomics.com.

The rate sets the bill. The calendar decides who earns the interest first.
